Our pledge to Jordan Pointe

Six promises every board member makes to every neighbor.

1

We decide in the open

Agendas posted ahead, votes recorded, minutes shared.

  • Agendas posted before every meeting
  • Decisions made only at meetings with a quorum, or by every director's signed consent
  • Minutes with each director's vote, by name, posted within 30 days
  • The manager's financial reports posted on the owner portal each quarter, and year-end statements sent to every owner
2

We ask you first

Owner polls before big decisions, and you decide when we're unsure.

  • Owner polls before new amenities, new rules and large expenses
  • A yearly priorities survey before the budget
  • Results published before the board votes, and reasons given if it goes another way
  • When the board is unsure or divided on something big, owners decide
  • Owners ratify the budget and vote on special assessments
3

We treat every home the same

Written standards, reminders before inspections, collections only as a last resort.

  • Objective, written standards, adopted with 15 days' notice
  • Seasonal community reviews from the street, announced ahead and the same for every home
  • Courtesy first, with notice and a hearing before any fine
  • Collections only as the last resort, after a payment plan is offered
  • No discrimination, ever
4

We're your neighbors first

Directors you can talk to, a board email, open forums.

  • Directors talk freely with owners; only legal, hearing, delinquency and personnel matters stay confidential
  • A direct board email and an official announcements channel
  • Open forums where owners are heard
  • Events, amenities and programs that bring the community together, like a group-rate lawn service
5

We follow through

A public to-do list, answers to every request, a signed code of conduct.

  • A public to-do list of what the board needs to fix
  • Records requests answered on a clock
  • Conflicts of interest disclosed, with those directors stepping aside
  • A code of conduct every director signs
6

We take care of what we share

Audits, a ratified budget, protected reserves, a well-chosen manager.

  • Annual independent audits and a ratified budget
  • Reserves kept for repair and replacement, shown by category
  • A management company chosen openly and held to clear standards
  • A yearly calendar so no duty is missed

What we expect from our management company

A partner that helps us succeed

A good management company works as one team with our board. It makes the board's job easier, not harder, and brings the same gentle, friendly touch our board shows every neighbor. We pay it to guide us, not just carry out instructions, so it speaks up before a mistake happens.

  • Advises before we act. Tells the board in writing when a proposed action or rule isn't in line with our covenants, Bylaws or state law, and suggests a way that is.
  • Finds our weak spots. Reviews the Declaration, Bylaws and Guidelines every year and recommends fixes before problems arise.
  • Lays out options and costs first. Explains what our documents already allow, the alternatives, and the likely cost before the board hires an attorney or starts an amendment.
  • Keeps us on schedule. Tracks every deadline: notices, budgets, audits, elections.
  • Cares about our neighbors. Answers questions and records requests promptly and kindly, keeps the portal current, and treats every neighbor with warmth and respect.
  • Is accountable to us. Meets written standards in its contract, with a yearly review shared with owners.

Had this been in place, the trash-can amendment would have started with a plain review of what the covenant already allows, and owners would have been asked before any legal fees were spent.

      Your first 100 days

      A path forward for a new board

      A new board inherits the problems in this guide, not the blame for them. The first months are the chance to reset trust: organize properly, listen before deciding, fix the foundations, then build. Each step links to the details.

      Weeks 1–2

      Organize

      • Elect officers by recorded vote at the first meeting.
      • Every director signs the code of conduct and discloses any ties to vendors or the manager.
      • Confirm the D&O insurance covers each director.
      • Adopt a communications policy: talk freely with neighbors, keep only legal, hearing, delinquency and personnel matters confidential.
      • Have the manager set up a board email address and an official announcements channel.
      • Send owners a fresh-start letter (template below).

      Days 15–30

      Listen and open up

      • Hold an open forum with no time limit on owner concerns.
      • Launch an owner priorities survey: amenities, fences, lawn program, enclosed cabana, pickleball.
      • Have the manager post current financials, minutes, and board and committee rosters on the portal.
      • Have the manager answer every outstanding records request in writing, including the attorney spending.
      • Pause enforcement of anything never properly adopted, and withdraw the winter mold and perennial notices.
      • Pause collections referrals until a written policy is in place.

      Days 30–60

      Fix the foundations

      • Ratify, by one recorded resolution, the past actions whose votes are at risk, or withdraw them.
      • Get the developer's written delegation of architectural authority on file.
      • Engage an independent CPA for the missing audits, and explain the reserve write-off.
      • Adopt written policies: enforcement, collections, records requests, owner input, communications.
      • Appoint committees through an open call, and publish rosters.
      • Start an open, scored search for a management company, with PPM invited.

      Days 60–100

      Build

      • Draft measurable maintenance standards and a seasonal review calendar with owners, then adopt them with 15 days' notice.
      • Share the survey results and act on the top priorities: poll owners on fences, set up the optional lawn program, study the cabana enclosure.
      • Prepare next year's budget from the reserve study and owner priorities, and send it for ratification.
      • Plan the annual meeting: nominating committee, secret ballot, statement of the board's acts.
      • Report back to owners: what the board said it would do, and what it did.

      Lessons learned

      What went wrong, and what to do instead
      What went wrongThe fix
      Deciding in private. Closed monthly meetings, decisions owners learned about afterward.
      The fixDecide only at meetings with a quorum. Post the agenda ahead, let owners speak, and post minutes with every vote within 30 days.
      "You voted for us so we decide for you."
      The fixAsk owners first on new amenities, new rules and large expenses, and publish the results before voting. The developer and PPM did this, and it worked.
      Online votes with no signed consents.
      The fixA vote outside a meeting counts only if every director signs a written consent, filed with the minutes. Otherwise, wait for a meeting.
      Rules enforced before they were adopted. The April 2025 list; Revision 06.
      The fixAdopt a rule by recorded vote, mail every owner a copy, and wait 15 days before enforcing it.
      Enforcement first, community second.
      The fixCourtesy first. Measurable written standards, letters that name the rule, and the Bylaws' notice and hearing before any fine.
      "Gotcha" notices. Winter mold letters to some homes; dormant perennials cited as weeds in the snow.
      The fixObjective standards, a published seasonal calendar, reminders to every home first, announced community reviews from the street, and cure periods that fit the season.
      Collections too early.
      The fixSeveral friendly reminders, a personal call, a payment plan or hardship option, and the legally required 15-day notice first. A board vote on each account before any referral.
      Records requests unanswered.
      The fixAcknowledge within 3 business days, answer within 10, and keep a log.
      Directors told not to talk to residents.
      The fixTalk with your neighbors. Keep only legal, hearing, delinquency and personnel matters confidential, and don't speak for the board alone.
      Personal ties shaping big decisions. The management company choice.
      The fixDisclose every relationship, step aside from those votes, and use an open, scored process.
      Seats and committees filled quietly.
      The fixAnnounce every opening, take applications, choose by recorded vote, and publish rosters with terms.
      Required duties skipped. No audits, no budget ratification, elections by acclamation.
      The fixFollow the yearly calendar below.
      Spending owners didn't expect. Cabana painting; cameras from reserves.
      The fixSurvey owners on priorities before the budget, keep reserves for repair and replacement, and publish bids and votes.

      Yearly calendar

      The fiscal year runs January to December
      1. JanuaryStart the audit

        The manager engages an independent CPA to audit the year just ended; the board approves the engagement.

      2. By mid-MarchYear-end statements to owners

        The manager sends every owner the prior year's income statement and balance sheet (state law sets a 75-day deadline; verify current text).

      3. SpringReserve study and pool opening

        The manager updates the reserve study with a reserve specialist and proposes repair and replacement projects for the board to approve.

      4. July to AugustOwner priorities survey; call for volunteers

        The manager sends the owner priorities survey and announces committee openings. The board appoints the nominating committee.

      5. SeptemberAdopt and mail the proposed budget

        The board adopts it; within 30 days the manager mails it to every owner with notice of a ratification meeting 10 to 30 days after mailing. It can be combined with the annual meeting.

      6. 10 to 60 days beforeAnnual meeting notice

        The manager mails written notice with the agenda, the seats up for election and any budget changes.

      7. NovemberAnnual meeting

        Secret-ballot election, the board's statement of its acts, the budget and income statement. Fix next year's assessment at least 20 days before January 1.

      8. DecemberNew board organizes

        Elect officers at the first board meeting after the annual meeting. Directors sign the code of conduct and disclose conflicts. The manager mails assessment notices at least 10 days before January 1. Confirm D&O insurance.

      9. Every meetingThe basics

        The manager posts the agenda ahead and the approved minutes within 30 days. The board allows owner comment time, records the quorum and counts every vote. The manager posts financial reports each quarter and brings the violation and records-request logs to each meeting.

      How to do it right

      Short playbooks for the jobs that went wrong
      Adopt or change a rule or guideline
      1. Confirm the authority: a covenant it interprets, or written delegation for architectural guidelines.
      2. Ask owners for comment on the draft.
      3. Vote at a meeting, recorded in the minutes.
      4. Mail every owner the final text.
      5. Enforce it only 15 days after mailing, and log the change.
      Spend money
      1. Check that it's in the ratified budget, or a repair or replacement the reserves cover.
      2. Get bids for anything significant.
      3. Ask owners first for new amenities or large unbudgeted costs.
      4. Vote at a meeting, recording the amount, vendor and fund.
      5. Report it in the treasurer's report.
      Decide something between meetings
      1. Use the written consent form below.
      2. Every director must sign; one holdout means it waits for a meeting.
      3. File the signed consents with the minutes, and list the action in the next meeting's minutes.
      Fill a seat or a committee
      1. The manager announces the opening to every owner, with the duties and how to apply.
      2. The manager acknowledges each application within 5 business days.
      3. The board chooses by recorded vote, using the published criteria.
      4. The manager tells every applicant the outcome in writing and posts the roster with terms.
      5. Removal, if ever needed, is by recorded board vote under a written policy, after the member can respond.
      Handle a violation
      1. Only during an announced, seasonal community review, looking only at what's visible from the street (exteriors, lawns and landscaping), after a reminder to all owners.
      2. Confirm it breaks a covenant or an adopted, measurable standard, with a dated photo and a second person's check.
      3. Send a courtesy letter naming the rule and the fix.
      4. Before any fine: the Bylaws' notice with 10+ days to request a hearing, with proof of delivery in the minutes.
      5. Hold the hearing if requested and send a written decision.
      6. Log it, and treat similar cases the same way.
      Help a neighbor who falls behind on dues
      1. Start with friendly reminders: a statement in the first 10 days, a second note around day 15.
      2. Around day 30, call to check in and offer a payment plan or hardship option.
      3. Around day 45, a third reminder with the payment options and who to call.
      4. Only then, the written 15-day notice state law requires before any fees, with a named person to call.
      5. Anything beyond that is a last resort, only after a board review and a separate vote on that specific account.
      Answer a records request or a petition
      1. Records: acknowledge in 3 business days; within 10, provide each item or say it doesn't exist; offer a time to inspect.
      2. Petition from 10% of owners for a special meeting: verify signatures and call the meeting promptly, with notice 10 to 60 days ahead stating its exact purpose.
      Work with the management company
      1. Let the manager handle first responses, logs, mailings, inspections and financial statements; the board decides and votes.
      2. Get a written summary before each meeting instead of reading every email together.
      3. Review the manager against the contract's service standards each year.
      4. Keep the contract cancellable on 90 days' notice, with records belonging to the association.

      What members decide

      The board runs the association day to day, and our documents set no dollar amount above which spending needs a member vote. But owners hold several decisions the board can't make alone, and several tools to be heard. Each item cites the page it comes from.

      Decisions that belong to the members

      DecisionWhat it takesSource
      The annual budgetThe board proposes; members can reject.The board must mail the proposed budget within 30 days of adopting it, with notice of a ratification meeting 10 to 30 days later. A majority of the entire membership can reject it; if assessments rise 10% or less, rejection takes 67%. A rejected budget falls back to the last ratified one.
      Special assessmentsExtra charges for capital improvements or one-time costsApproval by a majority of the owners' votes.
      Electing directorsAt the annual meeting, by secret written ballot; the most votes wins.
      Removing a directorMajority vote of members at a meeting called for that purpose.
      Number of directorsSet by a vote of the members (3, 5 or 7, up to 9).
      Amending the BylawsMembers can add rules the board can't undo.Two-thirds of the votes cast at a members' meeting with a quorum (10% of the votes). A bylaw the members adopt can't be changed by the board unless it says so.
      Amending the covenants80% member vote, or an instrument signed by owners of at least 75% of lots.
      Borrowing against association property80% of the votes.
      Transferring common area80% of the votes.

      What the board decides alone

      • Spending within the ratified budget, including repairs and projects paid from reserves, such as repainting. There is no dollar cap.
      • Hiring contractors, the management company and attorneys.
      • Adopting rules, as long as every owner gets a written copy 15 days before they take effect.
      • Even then, every decision needs a board vote at a meeting with a quorum, or written consent signed by every director, and it belongs in the minutes you can inspect.

      Board meetings and the open forum

      • State law: the board must, at regular intervals, let owners attend a portion of a board meeting and speak about their concerns. It may place reasonable limits on the number of speakers and on speaking time. Meetings follow Robert's Rules of Order unless the Bylaws say otherwise. (N.C.G.S. 47F-3-108(b) and (c), verify current text.)
      • Our Bylaws don't require board meetings to be open, so a closed meeting with a set owner-comment period generally meets the minimum. Whether a fixed 30 minutes is a "reasonable" limit depends on how many owners want to speak.
      • What the board has said: in 2025 it described quarterly open sessions, with concerns sent to the management company 7 days ahead.
      • What still applies to closed meetings: decisions made there must be recorded in the minutes, members may inspect the minutes and records, and the board must present a statement of its acts at the annual meeting, or at a special meeting when owners with one-fourth of the votes request it in writing 5 working days ahead.

      Formal demand to inspect records

      For when informal requests are refused
      • Members may inspect the association's books and records . Under NC nonprofit law, a member may also inspect its accounting records, including invoices and contracts, by giving written notice at least 5 business days ahead, in good faith, describing the records and the purpose (N.C.G.S. 55A-16-02, verify current text).
      • If the association refuses without a good-faith reason, a court can order the inspection and generally must make the association pay the member's costs, including attorney's fees (N.C.G.S. 55A-16-04).
      • Several owners sending the same demand, each signed, carries more weight than one.

      A. Notice and member approval for large expenses
      Article VII, Section 4. Large Expenditures. (a) Before committing the Association to any single expenditure or contract of more than $10,000 that is not specifically listed in the budget most recently ratified by the Members or in the Association's current reserve study, the Board shall give every Member written notice at least thirty (30) days in advance, describing the work, its estimated cost, the bids received, and the fund to be charged, and shall allow Members to comment at a Board meeting before the vote. (b) Any such expenditure or contract of more than $25,000 shall also require approval by a majority of the votes cast at a regular or special meeting of the Members at which a quorum is present. (c) This Section does not apply to repairs needed to address an immediate threat to health, safety or property. The Board shall report any such emergency expenditure to the Members in writing within fifteen (15) days. (d) This Section was adopted by the Members and may be amended or repealed only by the Members.
      B. Open board meetings
      Article VI, Section 6. Open Meetings. (a) All regular and special meetings of the Board of Directors shall be open to Members, except that the Board may meet in executive session to consult with the Association's attorney, discuss personnel or contract negotiations, hold violation hearings, or discuss delinquent accounts. No vote shall be taken in executive session except on those matters, and the general subject of every executive session shall be recorded in the minutes. (b) The agenda for each regular Board meeting shall be sent or posted to Members at least five (5) days in advance. Members may speak on any agenda item before the Board votes on it, subject to reasonable time limits. (c) Draft minutes of each Board meeting, including every motion and how each director voted, shall be made available to Members within thirty (30) days. (d) This Section was adopted by the Members and may be amended or repealed only by the Members.

      How owners can act

      1. Call a special meeting. Owners holding 10% of the votes, about 44 homes, can require one in writing. The notice must include the exact purpose and the text of any proposal.
      2. Vote on the budget. Ask for the ratification meeting and vote to reject a budget you don't support.
      3. Inspect the records. Contracts, bids, invoices and minutes. If informal requests are refused, make a formal written demand, described above.
      4. Amend the Bylaws. At that meeting, a quorum of 10% and two-thirds of votes cast can adopt a bylaw the board can't undo.
      5. Vote in board elections and, if needed, remove a director.

      Before a vote on the drafts

      • Attorney review, including any conflict with the Declaration, which controls over the Bylaws
      • Pick the dollar amounts
      • Petition for a special meeting
      • Notice with the full text, 10 to 60 days ahead
      • Proxies collected to reach quorum

      Homeowner committees

      Committees serve at the pleasure of the board, each with a board liaison. Members should be in good standing on dues, and committees cannot spend funds without prior board approval. Each committee reports at the monthly board business meeting and contributes to the newsletter.
